## 4.2 Case study 1: a simple analytical strategy based on reduced EEG features

### 4.2.1 Research question and EEG features
The first case study addresses the following research question: are the amplitude and latency of specific ERP components associated with neuropsychological performance and demographic characteristics in post-stroke patients? This question is hypothesis-driven in nature, in that it targets specific, theoretically motivated aspects of the EEG signal, namely two well-characterised ERP components, rather than exploring the full spectral or spatiotemporal structure of the data.
As introduced in Section 3.1, ERP components are voltage deflections time-locked to a stimulus or response, whose amplitude and latency are thought to reflect specific cognitive processes. In the present case study, two components are of interest. The N400 is a negative deflection peaking approximately 400 ms after the onset of a semantically incongruent or unexpected stimulus, and is widely used as a neural index of semantic processing and language comprehension (Kutas & Federmeier, 2011). The P300 is a positive deflection peaking approximately 300 ms after a task-relevant target stimulus, and is associated with attentional resource allocation and working memory updating (Polich, 2007). Both components have well-established functional interpretations and have been extensively studied in neurological populations, including stroke patients, making them well suited to a hypothesis-driven analytical approach. 
For each participant, two features are extracted per component: amplitude, computed as the mean voltage within a predefined time window centred on the expected peak latency of the component (e.g., 350–500 ms for the N400; 250–600 ms for the P300, a deliberately wide window chosen to accommodate the delayed latencies frequently observed in clinical populations), averaged across a set of theoretically motivated electrodes (centro-parietal sites for the P300; centro-parietal sites, often slightly right-lateralised, for the N400); and latency, defined as the time point of maximum absolute amplitude within the same window and electrode set. This yields four participant-level summary features, N400 amplitude, N400 latency, P300 amplitude, and P300 latency, which constitute the neural predictors entering the statistical analysis.
This feature extraction procedure exemplifies the dimensionality reduction strategy described in Section 3.1: the high-dimensional EEG signal, consisting of hundreds of electrodes sampled at hundreds of time points, is reduced to four interpretable scalar values per participant. The resulting dataset, combined with the neuropsychological scores and demographic variables described in Section 4.1, takes the form of a compact participant × variable matrix suitable for classical statistical modelling.
Two clarifications are in order regarding these features. First, ERP components such as the N400 and P300 can only be obtained from a task-based recording, in which the EEG is time-locked to repeated, controlled stimuli (for example, a semantic-priming or sentence-congruency paradigm for the N400, and an oddball paradigm for the P300). They cannot be derived from the resting-state recording that provides the spectral data of Case Study 2 (Section 4.1.3). In a real study, the two case studies would therefore draw on two distinct acquisitions: a task-based session for the ERP features and a resting-state session for the spectral analysis. Second, because the dataset used here is a mock dataset (Section 4.1.3), the four ERP features were not extracted from an actual task recording but simulated; they serve only to illustrate the analytical workflow, and the time windows and electrode sites specified above are provided as realistic examples rather than as recording or analysis specifications.

### 4.2.2 Analytical approach
To relate the extracted ERP features to neuropsychological outcomes and demographic variables, a regression-based framework is adopted. The choice of specific model depends on the nature of the outcome variable. For continuous outcomes, such as the simulated cognitive scores or the CRIq composite, standard linear regression is appropriate, provided that model assumptions (linearity, homoscedasticity, approximate normality of residuals) are adequately checked. For ordinal or count outcomes, which arise whenever a clinical scale is analysed on its native ordinal metric rather than as a continuous score, a generalised linear model (GLM) with an appropriate link function (e.g., Poisson or ordinal logistic) may be more suitable.
In the simplest formulation, each neuropsychological score is regressed onto one or more ERP features, with relevant covariates included to account for potential confounding. For example, one might model the MoCA score as a function of P300 amplitude and latency, controlling for age, sex, and cognitive reserve (CRIq). Similarly, APACS scores indexing pragmatic language comprehension might be regressed onto N400 amplitude, given the established role of the N400 in semantic and pragmatic processing. In the present simplified dataset, the available covariates are age, sex, and CRIq; in a real study, additional clinical covariates such as lesion site or time onset would further help to disentangle neural effects from clinical heterogeneity (see Section 4.1.1).
Because dependency in the EEG signal has been handled upstream, through the aggregation of electrode- and time-point-level data into four scalar features, the statistical model itself operates under the assumption of independence at the subject level. This assumption is approximately satisfied given that each participant contributes a single set of values. However, several practical issues warrant attention.
First, missing data are common in clinical neuropsychological datasets, arising from fatigue, incomplete testing, or participant dropout. Depending on the extent and pattern of missingness, different strategies may be appropriate: listwise deletion (excluding participants with any missing value) is simple but reduces sample size and may introduce bias if data are not missing completely at random; multiple imputation preserves sample size and produces less biased estimates under the weaker assumption that data are missing at random, and is generally preferred in clinical samples.
Second, multicollinearity among predictors, for instance, between age and cognitive reserve, or between different neuropsychological scores, can inflate standard errors and destabilise model estimates. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) should be inspected before interpreting regression coefficients, and highly collinear predictors should either be combined (e.g., via composite scores) or entered in separate models.
Third, when multiple regression models are run simultaneously, for instance, one model per neuropsychological outcome, the issue of multiple comparisons arises. Appropriate correction procedures (e.g., Bonferroni correction or false discovery rate control) should be applied to limit the risk of spurious findings.

### 4.2.3 Strengths and limitations
This approach offers several advantages. By reducing EEG data to a small number of theoretically grounded features, it enables the use of well-established statistical models that are widely understood in the neuropsychological community. The resulting analyses are straightforward to implement, computationally efficient, and easy to interpret and communicate, including to clinical audiences unfamiliar with EEG methodology. The reliance on established ERP components, whose functional interpretation is supported by decades of empirical research across neurological populations, further strengthens the face validity and interpretability of the results.
However, these advantages come at the cost of strong a priori decisions. The choice of time windows, electrode sets, and component definitions determines which aspects of the neural signal are retained and which are discarded. If the neural correlates of the behaviour of interest do not conform to the predefined features, for instance, if the relevant effect unfolds outside the selected time window, or is distributed across electrodes not included in the region of interest, the analysis will fail to detect it. This approach may therefore overlook distributed, temporally extended, or atypical neural patterns, which are particularly likely in clinical populations such as stroke patients, where the spatial and temporal profile of ERP components may deviate substantially from normative expectations.
Moreover, simplifying the neural data does not eliminate all sources of analytical complexity. Behavioural variables may still differ substantially in scale and distributional properties, requiring careful model selection and assumption checking. Missing data and collinearity among predictors can further affect model stability. The reduction of EEG data to amplitude and latency values also discards information about trial-to-trial variability, which may itself carry clinically relevant information, for instance, increased intraindividual variability in P300 latency has been associated with attentional dysregulation in neurological patients.
Overall, this strategy is most appropriate when the research question targets specific, hypothesis-driven brain–behaviour relationships and when interpretability and communicability are primary goals. It provides a transparent and accessible entry point for combining neural and behavioural data in neuropsychological research. When the research question is broader or more exploratory, for instance, when the neural correlates of interest are not well localised in time or space, or when the full spectral structure of the EEG signal is of interest, a more flexible approach is required, as illustrated in Case Study 2.
